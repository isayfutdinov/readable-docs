# Form rules

These rules decide what the reader meets first, in what order, and how a figure, a person or a gap looks. They apply to the plan and the draft; the check at the end walks them.

## The build: the reader's way, not a pile of facts

The text follows the way the reader goes to the result we want from them: the decision, the action, the understanding, the reply. List the steps this reader takes to get there, in the order they take them; the first lines give what the reader needs first, then the text goes from the general to the particular. Every section is one step of that way and its heading names the step ("Comparing the options", "Before you start", "Why frost cracks fresh concrete"). Every step needs facts from the sources: a step they have nothing for is skipped or becomes an open question, and an example, a limit, a takeaway or a next step they do not give is never written from general knowledge.

Two examples of a way (illustrations, not sections to fill):

- *A reader who chooses between options:* what happened and what is decided by when; what the choice comes down to; the options compared in one table; what the table means for the choice; what to approve and what follows.
- *A reader who performs work:* what is done and what the result is; what must be true before step 1; the steps in order, each with its actor and, when the action alone does not show it, what it is for; what to do when a step fails.

A section of facts of one kind — the incidents, the requirements, the dates, how things work, the arithmetic, a chronology — makes the reader carry each fact to the step where it matters: break it up and put each fact where the reader uses it. A fact used in two steps stands in full in the first and is named in the second, except a fact that belongs to a gathered answer or answers an obstacle: it stands in full there, and earlier steps name it. Calculations, volumes, the origin of estimates and background detail that would interrupt the argument go into one appendix after it (Devices); in a text without one, a figure's origin is a clause where it is used.

**One answer, one place.** Whatever this reader takes away as one answer stands in one place: the options against each other, where a mechanism holds and where it stops, what exactly to build, what to do first and what each step is for when a source says. Facts spread over sections leave the reader to assemble the answer; gather them. Where the reader compares, or gathers the answer from two or more facts, that place may end with a closing line (Devices).

**When the text asks for a yes** (an invitation, an approval, adoption). The text opens with the known facts that meet this reader's criteria for saying yes — never with a gap, a weak fact or background. What would make the reader refuse — a criterion the author does not meet, an objection, a risk — stands after them, in one item or paragraph with the known facts that remove or weaken it, so the reader meets both together; then the text goes on to what the reader does next and never closes on what the author lacks. Fictional example: a supplier's bid opens with the delivery times and the pilot results the tender scores; then "the tender asks for a local office, which we do not have; our engineers reach any site in the region within a day, as the three pilots showed"; then whom to contact for the site visit. Where no known fact answers the obstacle, phase 0 asks the author for one; unanswered, the obstacle is stated plainly, the text still goes on to what the reader does next, and the cycle report names it. In a comparison the table carries both sides; this rule adds no counterweight to an option, and a text where the reader chooses stays without a lean.

**People.** A person who acts, decides or is to be contacted is named so that this reader can tell who it is: name and role at first mention, then one designation everywhere, the open-questions table included (`../../review-text/references/readability-rules.md`, 1e). When the text speaks in the author's voice about work done with others, the author is such a person: their own part in each result — what they did, decided or led, apart from the team — is stated; where no source gives it, it is a question (`question-format.md`, Never dropped).

**The reader's words.** Teams, systems, fields, documents and events use terms this reader understands in context; the words of the materials are the author's context, not the reader's. An internal field is called by what it holds ("the delivery date"; `dlv_dt` in the part for the developer who will use it); a team's word for a thing is replaced by the reader's word for it. Where the reader must type, search for or quote an identifier (a host, a file, a command, a field in a form), the exact identifier stays next to the reader's word. An unfamiliar term is explained where it first appears, or in a glossary (Devices).

**Explicit meaning.** Name the actor, referent, relation and order that this reader needs, so the wording has one reading. The reader may combine facts the text states; they must not supply an unstated link or choose between readings to make the text work. An explanation makes its mechanism explicit without becoming a full instruction. Stating an unknown explicitly does not make the wording ambiguous. When the unclear wording is the source's own, the text does not rewrite it into one reading: `sourcing-rules.md`, What you may do with facts.

## Devices, and when they do a job

- **Glossary** — when the text uses several terms this reader would not know and defining them where they first appear would break the sentences; otherwise each term is defined at its first use. Never already explained references or plain words.
- **Status line** — in a working text: "Status: draft; questions N, M are needed for the decision (or the steps), the rest only for later work; questions at the end" while questions are open, "Status: ready" when none is, in the text's language. Never in a final text.
- **Why now** — when a deadline or a window drives the decision or the action; otherwise none.
- **What it comes down to** — when the text compares options or sets out steps: a few plain lines on the first screen with the questions or differences that decide, the order of the work, or the one constraint that shapes it. Only from sourced facts or arithmetic on them; no ranking, and a recommendation only when the task asks for one and a source or an answer gives it, then attributed (SKILL.md, Hard constraints). Fictional example: "The choice comes down to one question: can the youngest pupils start ten minutes later? If yes, city bus passes fit the budget; if not, only our own minibus does, and buying it needs approval this term."
- **Closing line** — under a comparison, or under a place that gathers the answer from two or more facts: what the reader would otherwise have to work out from those facts themselves — a difference, a condition, a case left uncovered — drawn from them. Never a cause, a forecast, a ranking, advice, a restated fact or a summary of what is known and what is not. Not under a section that states one thing, a table of steps, a list of contacts or places: they end with their last fact.
- **Overview** — when the reader needs the whole before the details or must find their part: a table of the parts or steps in order (who, what each needs), or the comparison table when the reader chooses. The parts below keep their actor and add what the table does not hold.
- **Table of open questions** — in a working text, when something waits on answers (Open questions).
- **Success criteria** — when the sources give them and the reader judges the result by them.
- **Comparison table** — when the reader chooses between options (below).
- **Appendix** — when figures need their derivation or background would interrupt the argument.

A template the user gives is a frame: each of its sections still needs a step and facts; a section with neither is left out and noted in the cycle report, never filled with general words.

## The first screen

The first screen (what the reader sees before scrolling; the first paragraph of a text shorter than a screen) gives the main thing for this reader in one to three sentences of prose before any table: what is decided and by when, what to do, the answer, the idea; in a text that asks for a yes, the facts that meet the reader's criteria (The build). A status line or an overview table does not replace it. Around it stand only the devices whose condition holds: the status line first, then why now, what it comes down to and the overview.

Not on the first screen: anything the reader must get through before the main thing — a paragraph that lists what the sections are ("below we describe…"), a description of the document or of the materials, the chronology of events, the state of the text in prose.

## The comparison table

When the reader chooses between options: rows are the options, "do nothing" included when it exists; the options the fork pairs stand next to each other. Columns: the option; how it works, in one line; what we get; what it costs and what we risk; what is unknown. How an option works comes from a source; where none says, the cell says it is not known (Status in words) and the question was asked in phase 0, never filled from general knowledge. Add a column only when every option fills it.

- A cell is a phrase or a few phrases joined by semicolons, taken in at a glance: the figure with its status in words ("about 30 000 to buy", "per the dealer"), the consequence against the alternative ("1 000 a month less than the coach"), which criterion it meets or misses.
- No source names, dates of sources or reasons in a cell beyond the status words; who gave an estimate and when goes into one note under the table or into the appendix.
- A point that is the same, or unknown, for every option is one sentence under the table, not a row or a repeated cell.
- Every risk or unknown that decides between options is in the table; a risk told only in prose elsewhere is missing from the comparison.

Fictional example:

| Option | How it works | What we get | What it costs and risks | Unknown |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hired coach | Nothing changes | All pupils on time, no new staff | 4 000 a month, over the transport budget | whether the operator adds a second morning run (question 3) |
| Own minibus | We buy a minibus and hire a part-time driver | 3 000 a month, 1 000 less than the coach; 16 seats for 20 pupils | about 30 000 to buy; per the dealer, delivery in six weeks | who drives when the driver is ill (question 4) |
| City bus passes | Pupils take the 7:40 bus with term passes | about 1 500 a month | the youngest pupils start ten minutes late and travel unaccompanied | whether parents accept unaccompanied travel (question 5) |

## Each figure once

A figure is stated in full only where the reader uses it: the comparison table (or the overview of an executor text), the sentence of the argument that uses it, and the appendix line that derives it. Everywhere else the text names it or states a difference or a consequence ("1 000 a month less", "pays back in two and a half years", "half the budget"). Never a status list that restates the table, a summary bullet and a section that restate each other, or a section that repeats the appendix's arithmetic. A derived figure is calculated once, with its inputs: in the appendix when the text has one, otherwise where the figure is used.

The same holds for a statement: a fact, a date, a name or a rule is stated in full once, where the reader acts on it, and named elsewhere. A section that only restates others, or holds only open questions, is not written.

## Status in words

Status travels with every figure (`sourcing-rules.md`), in words the reader does not have to decode:

- a fact or a decision stated by a source that can know it: no status word;
- code or configuration: "as implemented (version/place)"; this describes the version read, never an approved requirement or a confirmation of production behaviour;
- an estimate or a hedge: "about" or "≈"; whose estimate and when stand once (`sourcing-rules.md`, Status travels with the fact);
- a party's own word about itself, a party's request or a proposal nobody confirmed: "per X", "X asks for", "X proposed, not decided";
- a commitment (a contract term, a minimum period): stated as the commitment ("the hall is booked until the end of the season"), never turned into a cost unless a source names one;
- unknown: "not known (question N)" or "not decided, see question N".

Status is never a sign in place of words — a question number standing alone, a symbol explained in a legend. A preference or a habit keeps its words wherever it stands; it never becomes an instruction.

## Two readers

A text for two readers opens with a short shared part: the purpose, the facts and the dates both need, once. Then each reader's part, in the order that reader acts; it does not restate the shared facts.

## Open questions

These rules apply to a working text (SKILL.md, Working or final text); a final text has none.

- **Count.** A question enters the text only when its answer would change what a reader understands, decides or does; otherwise it goes to the cycle report with the reason. Merging: `question-format.md`, the gate, test 4. A gap the materials or the answers settle is not written.
- **At the end, split by what they stop.** After the last section (and before or after the appendix, once), in two groups: the questions that stop the decision or the action now, and those that only stop later work. Columns: number, question, who decides, what it blocks; a deadline column only when some source gives a deadline. Every row has the same form; who decides: `question-format.md`, Who decides. Mandatory columns are not a reason to invent an addressee or a date. The status line names the first group.
- **The body states what is known** as plain statements. It carries no pointer at the end of a sentence, no "Blocked until…" line on a step or section that does not wait, no sentence about the state of the questions. A sentence that mixes known and unknown is split in two: "X is known. Y is not known." In a table cell an unknown is written in status words (Status in words).
- **Dependent steps are grouped, not marked one by one.** The steps that can be done now stand apart from the steps that wait for named questions (one heading names the questions once). When the blocked steps sit in the middle of a sequence and the order matters, keep one ordered list with one line above it that names the waiting steps and their questions; in a list or prose the waiting step itself says in one clause that it waits for question N. In a table of steps the cell holds the status words ("not known (question N)"), never "waits for question N".
- **Executor texts.** While a question that blocks a step is open, the text is a draft: the status line says so, the waiting steps are grouped, the table says what must be answered. It is handed over as a draft, never as ready.

## Gaps in steps

A step whose input, check or failure path is unknown says what is not known, in one sentence, and the table carries the question. An unknown is never turned into a step of its own ("confirm that X works" when nobody said how). An interim action ("until then do X", "if the plaster cracks, stop") is written only when a source or an answer gives it. The text never fills a gap with a precaution of its own: an extra check, a wait, a test, a handover note, a "stop".

## Water (padding)

Write what a careful person writes for this reader from the same facts: choose, order and merge. Do not retell each material in turn. A detail that changes nothing this reader needs to understand, decide or do for the result we want from them stays out; a fact that does stays in (SKILL.md, Nothing known is lost). A block that repeats another — a "who does what" list after a plan table with actors, a summary that restates a table — is cut. There is no length target.

## The check

Walk it on the plan before the draft (the items a plan can show), on the draft, and after the fixes of each round of checks. For each item, the section named holds the rule. Rewrite what fails; do not add a note.

1. **The way** (The build): the headings, read in order, are this reader's steps to the result; every step has a section and every section is a step; no section of one kind of fact makes the reader carry its facts to a later step; the plan names the known content and sources of each row.
2. **One answer, one place** (The build; Devices, Closing line).
3. **The opening** (The first screen; When the text asks for a yes).
4. **Devices** (Devices): each has a job for this reader; no template section filled with general words.
5. **The comparison** (The comparison table): one table, how each option works in its row or as a question, what the table means for the choice said under it.
6. **Figures and statements** (Each figure once).
7. **Status** (Status in words; `sourcing-rules.md`): every qualifier of a source in its words; a party's request never stands as decided.
8. **People** (People): each person recognisable; the author's part stated in a text in the author's voice.
9. **Words and order** (The reader's words; Explicit meaning; Rules from review-text): nothing needed to understand a section comes after it.
10. **Open questions** (Open questions; `question-format.md`).
11. **Two readers** (Two readers).
12. **Water** (Water).
13. **The goal** (SKILL.md, Hard constraints): every section leads to the result we want from this reader and none works against it.
