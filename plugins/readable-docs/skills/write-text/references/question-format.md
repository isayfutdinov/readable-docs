# Questions

## To whom

Questions go to the user only. When the answer lies with someone else, the user chooses: the question becomes part of the text (an open question with that person as the one who decides), or the user finds out and brings the answer as a material. The skill never addresses a third party itself. The user is the addressee of the batch; that alone does not make the user the one who decides the open questions in the text (Who decides, below).

## When to ask and when to stop

- **A new text from materials.** Every gap goes through the gate (below). When at least one gap that passes blocks a reader task — without its answer the reader cannot decide, act or reply, or the text would state something wrongly — all the gaps that pass go to the user in one batch, written to `questions.md` next to the planned text, and the work stops until the answers come, unless the request says to write at once. A never-dropped kind always counts as blocking. When no passing gap blocks a reader task, there is no batch: write the text; the gaps go into the cycle report, and in a working text those that pass the Count of `form-rules.md` become open questions.
- **`revise`, or polishing a text.** No batch by default. A remark becomes a question only when it needs a new fact that passes the gate and blocks a reader task.
- **One batch per text.** Gaps found later become open questions, or are dropped by the gate. Unanswered, a gap becomes an open question of a working text when it passes the Count; in a final text only the part of the statement that needs the missing fact is left out, and the cycle report lists it.

## One question — one fact

Each question has four labelled parts, in the language of the request:

1. **What is unclear** — the fact, with its place in the materials (quote or reference), or that the materials do not mention it.
2. **Why it matters** — which section, decision or step it blocks; what would be written wrongly without it.
3. **Options**, if you see any — each with its consequence. Never with a default: "I propose to assume…" or "if you don't answer, I'll recommend…" is a decision taken for the user.
4. **Without an answer** — what the text will say: an open question, with who decides (below); in a final text, which part of the statement is left out. Never what you will choose.

One number holds one fact. A question with sub-questions ("who, when and how?") is several questions and counts as several. The batch has no cap: never-dropped kinds first, then the rest grouped by reader task, then questions the text can be written without. Number them so the user can answer in a line: "1: yes, 2: see the thread, 3: don't know". Ask about the status of a hedged or proposed fact when the reader's task depends on it; otherwise the status travels with the fact.

## The gate: what is not asked

Before a gap becomes a question or an open question, it passes four tests:

1. **Do the materials or the answers already settle it?** Search them. Only a material or an answer that states the fact settles it; then it is no question and no open question: use the fact. A fact a material states in other words is settled; a fact that only follows from what a material states is not. Not settled by: your inference of how the system, the process or the person probably works ("the shop closes at six, so an evening delivery never meets customers"); the request, when it speaks of the text and not of the work.
2. **Could the answer change what a reader understands, decides or does?** Name the reader task and the sentence an answer would add or change. Drop the gap only when you cannot name a sentence this reader needs for the result we want from them that some answer would add or change, and list it in the cycle report with that reason. Pure curiosity is not asked. Not reasons to drop: "the text can say it is unknown"; "the materials do not ask for it"; "the status already travels with the figure"; "another fact already shows it matters"; "the batch is long". When unsure, keep the gap. A never-dropped kind is never dropped here.
3. **Does a source give a reason to doubt what another source states plainly?** A condition, an agreement, a decision or a status that a source states plainly is not doubted: whether it is accepted, implemented, still valid or known to others is a gap only when a source gives a reason — a conflict between sources, a later message, a hedge, a party's own word. Without such a reason it is no question and no open question. If such a question was asked anyway and the answer is "I don't know", the text states the source's statement as it stands and adds no open question, hedge or "not known whether…" about it.
4. **Does another open question with the same decider and the same blocked task already cover it?** In the text, merge them into one open question; gaps that share only the decider, or only the blocked task, stay separate. In the batch a number still holds one fact: merge there only what one answer settles.

**Never dropped.** A gap is never dropped when the reader's decision or action rests on the fact whether or not any material mentions it — what the result must satisfy, how it will be judged, how the thing the reader relies on works, who must act or approve, what binds the reader. Such a gap is always asked and comes first in the batch. Examples:

- *When the reader chooses between options and a source only names an option:* how each option works. A label ("a shared rota") is not a mechanism.
- *When the reader performs an operation:* who approves it and whom to tell. Whoever asked for the text has not thereby approved the work.
- *When the text speaks in the author's voice about work done with others:* the author's own part in each result — what they did, decided or led, apart from the team.

Not asked at all: how to present what the sources settle (a gap the reader's decision depends on is stated, not asked about), and history behind a settled point when the reader needs only the point.

Example (fictional):

> **3. How long are backups kept?**
> What is unclear: the meeting notes say "keep them about a month" (notes, item 4); no period was decided.
> Why it matters: section 2 "Retention" and the storage cost in section 4 depend on it.
> Options: 30 days — fits the current storage; 90 days — needs more storage, cost not estimated.
> Without an answer: section 2 says the period is not decided, with an open question; who decides: not determined.

## Open questions in the text

Where the answer did not come, a working text keeps it in one table at the end; its columns and place: `form-rules.md`, Open questions. Write the column labels in the text's language, for example:

| № | Question | Decides | Blocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | How long are backups kept? | not determined | section 2 "Retention", the storage cost in section 4 |

A risk with no answer in the sources, or a step whose failure path is unknown, is a row of this table, not a paragraph of prose, when an answer would change what a reader understands, decides or does; otherwise it goes to the cycle report. The question never carries an interim action of the writer's own (`form-rules.md`, Gaps in steps).

### Who decides

- The one who decides is the person or body a source or an answer names for that point, written so that this reader recognises them: the same designation the text uses for that person elsewhere, with name and role at first mention (`form-rules.md`, People).
- When the sources give only a role, the text ties it to a person with what the sources say about them — for example, "the duty manager who booked the hall" — so that the reader cannot take them for someone else with a similar role.
- When the user puts a point off, the user decides it, named as this reader knows them — by name and role in the work as the sources give it, never "the user" or "the author".
- When a source says that person will not be available when the answer is needed (leave, departure, a handover), the cell says so, and names who decides instead when a source does; otherwise who decides in the meantime is itself an open question.
- Where no source or answer names anyone: "not determined", never the user or the author by default. One shared line for several questions ("to be settled at the meeting") is not an addressee, and a deadline you derived is not a deadline.

## A text that is a list of questions

A list of questions to another party is itself a text with a reader: the person who answers. It follows the same format, with one opening paragraph on what the answers are for and what depends on them.
